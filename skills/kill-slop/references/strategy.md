# Strategy and information architecture

For every mode, identify the interface type and primary user task or conversion action first. Do not choose fonts, cards, or animation while the main goal remains unclear.

## 1. Understand the context

Confirm or make reasonable assumptions about:

- Target audience, use case, and task or problem.
- Interface type, traffic source, visitor awareness, or current product context.
- Primary user action, conversion goal, or successful task completion.
- User objections, operational constraints, and current state.
- Available product screenshots, real data, interface states, cases, third-party validation, credentials, and risk reducers.

Interface goals differ by type:

- **Landing page**: one audience, one core offer, one primary conversion action.
- **Homepage**: multiple audiences and secondary paths are allowed, but one primary CTA must remain clear.
- **Product interface**: prioritize the user's task, current state, data, and next action rather than marketing claims.
- **Documentation or content page**: prioritize the reader's question, comprehension, and next useful action.

Ask with a recommendation when a directional decision is unclear. Decide reversible local details directly.

## 2. Write the core message and interface copy

For marketing, use one sentence to explain who the product is for, the situation in which they use it, how it works, and the result they receive.

For product UI, make the current task, object, state, units, and next action clear. Do not force a value proposition or promotional CTA into a working interface.

Headlines state a conclusion, concrete difference, object, or task. Supporting copy explains the mechanism, evidence, state, or limitations. Action labels use specific verbs and set accurate expectations.

Avoid:

- Empty jargon such as empower, seamless, extreme, next generation, redefine, or unlock.
- Repeated "not X, but Y" phrasing used only for cadence.
- Eyebrows, subtitles, numbering, periods, badges, or labels added only to balance a layout.
- Unsourced numbers, fake logo walls, fake testimonial portraits, invented metrics, and generated FAQ filler.
- Rephrasing the same claim or instruction across multiple regions.

Do not create choppy sentences, fake personality, excessive casualness, or a new formula merely to avoid AI slop.

## 3. Give every region one distinct job

Do not automatically apply Hero, Features, Pricing, FAQ, stat cards, or a three-column feature grid. Organize regions around user decisions or task flow. Possible regions include:

- A real task, object, workflow, and outcome.
- The core product interface, current state, or working data.
- Benefits, use cases, operational constraints, and limitations.
- Cases, third-party validation, data, and credentials when claims need support.
- Delivery, trial conditions, and risk reduction.
- Genuine user questions or recovery guidance.
- A final CTA for marketing, or the next logical action for product UI.

Every region must add new information or support a distinct task. Merge or remove repeated regions instead of padding the interface.

Use FAQ only for genuine questions, with no fixed item count. Put detailed content on secondary pages or in drawers and modals when appropriate.

## 4. Choose layouts by comprehension and task cost

- **Classic hero plus sections**: a real interface or demo explains the product well.
- **Long-form narrative**: the offer requires context, trust building, or objection handling.
- **Minimal conversion page**: visitors already have intent, or the offer is simple, such as a download, demo, or waitlist.
- **Comparison page**: visitors are evaluating alternatives or competitors.
- **Task workspace**: product users need a clear object, current state, primary action, and supporting detail.
- **Reader-focused content layout**: documentation needs navigation, searchable structure, examples, and readable prose.

These layouts are options, not templates. Follow the user's decision or task path when the content requires another structure.
